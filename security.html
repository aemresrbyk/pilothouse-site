Pilothouse

Security

Last updated: 17 November 2026 · How remote control works, what can reach your computer, and what we can never see

In short: remote control is off until you turn it on. Only devices you pair with a one-time QR code can reach your computer. Through the relay, every message is end-to-end encrypted, so neither the relay nor we can read or change it. Risky commands need an extra confirmation, a prompt that changed is never approved by an old tap, and every remote action is logged on your computer.

What can reach your computer

  • Nothing, by default. Remote control and the local control API are both off until you switch them on in Settings.
  • Through the relay (relay.usepilothouse.com, a Cloudflare Worker): your computer opens an outgoing connection. No port is opened on your network.
  • Over Tailscale: the computer listens only on its Tailscale address, never on your Wi-Fi or LAN, and checks the Host header against DNS rebinding.

Pairing

  • Each QR code pairs exactly one device and expires after 10 minutes.
  • You confirm every pairing. The phone shows a 6-digit security code; your computer shows the same code, the device’s name and its approximate location (city, from the relay) and asks Allow or Deny. Someone with a photo of your QR shows a different code, so you see it and deny.
  • The computer stores only a hash of each device’s token. Remove a device any time in Settings → Remote control, and it is locked out at once.
  • View-only links let someone watch; the computer refuses every action from them.

Approving from a phone, safely

  • Risky commands (rm -rf, force-push, git reset --hard, deploys, sudo, curl | sh, secrets such as .env) take two taps, and Face ID on the iPhone.
  • No stale taps. Each prompt carries a signature. If the agent’s question changed since you looked, your tap is refused instead of approving something else.
  • Auto-approve rules never cover risky commands, whatever the rules say.
  • Audit log. Every remote action is written to ~/.pilothouse/remote-audit.jsonl on your computer.

What Pilothouse never touches

Your agents keep their own logins and talk to their own providers; Pilothouse doesn’t sit in between and never reads their credentials. Every flag that lets an agent act without asking (such as --dangerously-skip-permissions) starts off and is shown in red when on.
